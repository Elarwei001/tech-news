## Headline Deep Dive

### Claude Breaks Cryptography Itself — Anthropic's AI Discovers Novel Mathematical Attacks on Post-Quantum and AES Algorithms

**Event**: On July 28, Anthropic published research showing that Claude Mythos Preview can find mathematical flaws in cryptographic algorithms — not implementation bugs in code, but weaknesses in the underlying mathematics that keeps online communications secure.

The first result is an improved key recovery attack against HAWK, a post-quantum digital signature scheme currently under consideration in NIST's third round of standardization. Despite HAWK having survived two years of expert human review, Mythos found a previously unexploited symmetry (a nontrivial automorphism) in the lattice structure HAWK relies on. This allows a faster enumeration attack that effectively cuts HAWK's key strength in half — HAWK-256's expected attack cost dropped from 2^64 to 2^38. The attack cost approximately $100,000 in API compute and 60 hours of work. The finding has been shared with HAWK's authors and disclosed to the NIST public mailing list.

The second result is an improved attack on 7-round reduced AES (the full cipher has 10 rounds). Mythos developed a technique it called a "Möbius Bridge" — a fingerprinting algorithm that eliminates one of the guesses an attacker needs to make, improving the speed of previous best attacks by 200-800×. This does not break full AES, but it advances the frontier of what's known about reduced-round variants that cryptographers study to estimate the full cipher's security margin.

Both results have no practical impact on production systems today. HAWK is not deployed, and the AES attack works only on a weakened variant. But they demonstrate something unprecedented: an AI model performing original cryptographic research that advances the state of the art.

**Link**: [Original](https://www.anthropic.com/research/discovering-cryptographic-weaknesses) | [中文翻译](../translations/2026-08-03/anthropic-discovering-cryptographic-weaknesses.md) *(AI 译)*

**Why it matters**: This is the first public demonstration of an AI model discovering novel mathematical attacks on cryptographic algorithms — as opposed to finding implementation bugs in code. The HAWK result directly impacts an ongoing NIST standardization process. And the discovery process itself is remarkable: Mythos worked semi-autonomously for the HAWK attack and almost entirely autonomously for AES, generating one billion output tokens over several days to develop the Möbius Bridge technique.

**Analysis**: Three things stand out.

First, the gap between AI discovering the attack and humans verifying it is revealing. It took Mythos one week to find the AES attack. It took two human researchers nearly a month to validate it. Anthropic explicitly states they are "reaching the limits of our own knowledge" — the AI is producing research that its own operators struggle to verify. This is a preview of a structural challenge: as AI-generated research accelerates, human verification becomes the bottleneck.

Second, the HAWK result has real consequences for the NIST post-quantum standardization process. Doubling key sizes eliminates HAWK's practical advantages as a PQC candidate. A candidate that survived two rounds of expert review was significantly weakened by 60 hours of AI compute. This may be the first case of AI input materially shaping a cryptography standard.

Third, Anthropic also partnered with ETH Zurich, Tel Aviv University, and TU Berlin to release CryptanalysisBench, a benchmark for evaluating LLM cryptanalytic capabilities. Combined with the attack results, this positions Anthropic as both the pioneer and the infrastructure provider for AI-assisted cryptography research. Expect every serious cryptography team to begin integrating AI tools into their review processes.

## OpenAI

### How GPT-5.6 Optimized Its Own Inference — OpenAI Details the Efficiency Flywheel

**Event**: On July 29, OpenAI published a detailed engineering report on how GPT-5.6 achieves its efficiency gains. The post reveals that GPT-5.6 Sol — the flagship model — was directly instrumental in optimizing the production systems used to serve it.

Three categories of improvement stand out:

**Kernel optimization**: GPT-5.6 Sol autonomously rewrote production GPU kernels in Triton and Gluon (OpenAI's open-source GPU programming languages), reducing end-to-end serving costs by 20%. OpenAI used FpSan, an open-source floating-point sanitizer, to verify correctness of AI-generated kernels.

**Speculative decoding**: Sol designed and ran hundreds of experiments to improve its own draft model (the smaller "speculator" model used in speculative decoding), increasing token-generation efficiency by more than 15%. It also autonomously monitored and intervened in the training process for the improved speculator, handling hardware failures and training instability.

**Workload-specific configuration**: The optimal serving configuration for inference depends heavily on workload characteristics — prompt length, batch size, cache hit rate, and more. Previously, the configuration space was too large to tune systematically. Sol analyzed production workloads, generated candidate configurations, and hyper-optimized the engine for each scenario.

The report also details the agentic harness — a Rust orchestration layer connecting models, tools, and user environments. Key design choices include deferred tool discovery (surfacing integrations only when needed), append-only context management (preserving prompt cache prefixes), and tool output capping at 10,000 tokens by default.

**Link**: [Original](https://openai.com/index/gpt-5-6-frontier-intelligence-efficiency/)

**Analysis**: This post is the most detailed look yet at how OpenAI uses its own models to improve its infrastructure. The image of a frontier AI model rewriting the kernels that run it — and then monitoring the training of its own faster speculative decoder — is striking. It's a concrete instance of the self-improvement flywheel that OpenAI described in its "Building Abundant Intelligence" essay: more capable models help engineers find efficiencies, which lower serving costs, which make the next generation more accessible.

The disclosure of Gluon as a GPU programming language maintained by OpenAI is notable — it suggests OpenAI is building its own compilation stack alongside NVIDIA's CUDA ecosystem. And FpSan, the floating-point sanitizer for verifying AI-written kernels, addresses one of the key trust questions: can you safely deploy code written by a model? OpenAI's answer is to build verification tooling alongside the generation capability.

### Scientific Computing in the Age of Agentic AI — Eight Case Studies

**Event**: On July 28, OpenAI published an exploratory field report of eight agent-assisted scientific computing projects, primarily in the life sciences. Five used Codex alone; three used a combination of Codex and Claude Code. The report identifies recurring themes across projects ranging from routine maintenance to GPU-native rewrites of bioinformatics tools.

Key findings from the case studies:

**Shift from implementation to verification**: Contributors consistently describe their role moving from writing code to specifying what to build, defining how to measure correctness, and deciding when results are ready. The bottleneck has shifted from producing code to validating it.

**The "last mile" problem**: Agents produce initial implementations quickly, but resolving edge cases and subtle numerical differences takes much longer. Completing the final stretch of an implementation often required the most human effort.

**Confidence without correctness**: Agents often expressed confidence even when their work contained clear errors. Human reviewers needed external references — exact output agreement, parity with existing tools, or statistical behavior — to reliably validate results.

**Stewardship gap**: Lower implementation costs make it easier to produce rewrites, but this risks fragmenting users and spreading expert attention. Several projects highlighted the tension between modernizing code and maintaining the institutional knowledge embedded in existing tools.

The report includes case studies on modernizing cyvcf2 (a Python library for genomic variant data), MHCflurry (a peptide-MHC binding predictor), and rustar-aligner (a GPU-native sequence alignment tool), among others.

**Link**: [Original](https://openai.com/index/scientific-computing-agentic-ai/)

**Analysis**: This is one of the most honest assessments of AI coding agents in scientific research to date. OpenAI doesn't oversell the capabilities — instead, it highlights the verification bottleneck, the last-mile problem, and the risk of producing abandoned rewrites. The finding that agents "often expressed confidence even when their work contained clear errors" is a critical data point for anyone deploying AI coding tools in high-stakes domains.

The case study format is effective: real projects, real researchers, real trade-offs. The tension between modernization and stewardship — "today's modern rewrite can become tomorrow's abandoned code" — is a genuinely novel insight that deserves more attention in the broader AI coding tools conversation.

## Trend Observation

**AI is beginning to produce knowledge its operators cannot fully verify.** Anthropic's cryptography research and OpenAI's scientific computing report converge on the same structural challenge: AI models are now generating research outputs (cryptographic attacks, scientific code) that require significant human effort to validate — and in some cases exceed the domain expertise of their operators. The HAWK attack was found in one week but took a month to verify. Scientific coding agents produce implementations quickly but "express confidence even when their work contained clear errors." Verification, not generation, is becoming the new bottleneck for AI-assisted research.

**The self-optimizing stack is taking shape.** OpenAI's efficiency report shows a model rewriting its own kernels, optimizing its own speculative decoder, and tuning its own serving configuration. This isn't hypothetical self-improvement — it's a concrete engineering workflow where the model is a participant in its own optimization. Expect this pattern to compound: each generation of models will be more effective at optimizing the infrastructure that serves them.

**Post-quantum cryptography is getting AI stress-tested.** Anthropic's HAWK result may be the first case of AI input materially shaping a NIST standardization decision. With CryptanalysisBench now available as a benchmark, expect more systematic AI-assisted review of cryptographic candidates — both before and after deployment. This is a positive development for security, even as it raises the stakes for the ongoing competition between attack capabilities and defensive design.
